    First time publishing about your teaching?

    Writing about a course you teach is a different kind of paper from your disciplinary research, but you already have most of what you need: a problem you noticed, a change you made, and some evidence of what happened. These steps get you from a good classroom idea to a submission.

    1. Decide what kind of paper you have

    • A teaching idea or activity that worked: a practitioner journal (marked “Practitioner journal or magazine”) or a teaching-materials journal such as CourseSource or MedEdPORTAL, where the lesson itself is the publication.
    • A change you studied with data on student learning or attitudes: a SoTL or discipline-based education research journal.
    • Early results or work in progress: a conference poster with a proceedings paper (PERC, ASEE work-in-progress papers, SIGCSE experience reports). These are reviewed, citable, and a common first step.

    2. Talk to your IRB before you collect data

    If you plan to publish anything drawn from your students (grades, survey answers, work samples, interviews), it is human subjects research, and your institution's Institutional Review Board (IRB) decides how it is reviewed. Many classroom studies qualify as exempt under the federal Common Rule category for research in established educational settings on normal educational practices, but only the IRB can make that call, and getting approval before you collect data is far easier than asking to use data afterward. A few habits make approval easy:

    • Apply before the term starts; exempt reviews are often quick, but not same-week.
    • Have someone other than the instructor collect consent, and hold consent forms until grades are in, so students do not feel pressured.
    • Remove names and IDs before analysis. Student records are also covered by FERPA (opens in a new tab).
    • Ask your teaching center: many have a template protocol for classroom research.

    Background: the Common Rule's exempt categories are at HHS Office for Human Research Protections (opens in a new tab). Rules outside the US differ; ask your ethics board.

    3. Treat your teaching as research

    The CIRTL Network (opens in a new tab) calls this teaching-as-research: define what you want students to learn, find out what is already known, design the change, collect evidence, and reflect and share. Its free online courses walk graduate students and postdocs through a full teaching-as-research project, and many member campuses run local versions. The same cycle is the skeleton of most SoTL papers.

    4. Find a call for papers

    • Special issues and themed calls are announced on journal home pages and in society newsletters; they are often more open to first-time authors.
    • Conference calls for posters and proceedings papers are in STEMEd+ Conferences, and their deadlines in STEMEd+ Deadlines.
    • Read a few recent articles in the venue you choose, and follow its article types and length limits closely.

    5. Get support

    • Writing groups, SoTL fellowships, and teaching-as-research programs are listed in STEMEd+ Faculty Development.
    • If an article fee is a barrier, ask the journal for a waiver before you submit, and ask your library: many have open-access agreements that cover fees.
    • Share a preprint or your materials in the STEMEd+ Commons collection (opens in a new tab) to get feedback and a citable DOI.
